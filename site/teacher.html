Teacher pack

Ready-to-use lessons about the robots and hardware NASA left on the Moon, Mars and across the solar system. Written for ages 8–14; each lesson takes about 45 minutes and works on classroom computers, tablets or a single projector. Every fact comes from the site's archive of NASA mission records, and the worksheets are generated from that same data.

Lesson plans

Lesson 1 · Earth & space science

Ghost hunt: where are they now?

Big idea: Spacecraft are still out there, on the Moon, on Mars and across the solar system, and each one has a story.

Students will: locate machines on a map using latitude and longitude; describe what one machine discovered; compare distances across the solar system.

  1. Hook (5 min): Show the Apollo 11 “Then & now” slider. Ask: “Do you think it's still there?”
  2. Explore (20 min): In pairs, students complete Worksheet 1: Ghost hunt using the Ghost Map, dossiers and the Elsewhere section.
  3. Share (10 min): Each pair presents one surprising fact. Play its “ghost log” aloud with .
  4. Wrap up (10 min): Run the Class quiz on the projector.

Standards links (NGSS): MS-ESS1-3 (scale properties of objects in the solar system).

Lesson 2 · Engineering design & forces

How do you land on another world?

Big idea: Engineers choose a landing method based on gravity, air and terrain. There is no single “best” way.

Students will: compare landing designs; explain why the Moon needs no parachute; test a landing in the game and evaluate their own design choices.

  1. Hook (5 min): Play the “Airbag bounce” and “Sky crane” animations from How it worked.
  2. Compare (15 min): Complete Worksheet 4: Landing methods.
  3. Test (15 min): Students play Land it yourself on the Moon, then Mars, and record their best landing speed and fuel left.
  4. Reflect (10 min): Why did Mars need the parachute? Why does Mars need more engine power than the Moon? Extension: egg-drop challenge with a budget of materials.

Standards links (NGSS): 3-5-ETS1-2 and MS-ETS1-2 (compare and evaluate design solutions); MS-PS2-4 (gravitational interactions depend on mass).

Lesson 3 · Data & storytelling

Planned vs. what really happened

Big idea: Many missions lasted far longer than planned, because engineers build in safety margins and teams solve problems creatively.

Students will: read a data table; calculate how many times longer a mission lasted; make a bar chart; write a first-person “ghost log”.

  1. Hook (5 min): Read Opportunity's ghost log aloud. Planned: 90 days. Actual: 14+ years.
  2. Data (20 min): Complete Worksheet 3: Planned vs. actual, including the bar chart.
  3. Write (15 min): Complete Worksheet 2: Write a ghost log for any machine in the archive.
  4. Share (5 min): Volunteers read their ghost logs aloud.

Cross-curricular: math (ratios, bar charts) and English (narrative writing from facts).

Tips

  • Mixed reading levels: the ⚙ settings switch every story between Explorer (ages 8–11) and Scientist (12+), change text size, and add dyslexia-friendly spacing. Every story can be read aloud.
  • No internet in class? Open the site once while online. It then works offline, and on tablets it can be installed as an app. (Videos and sounds still need internet.)
  • Myth-busting: the site corrects common myths, such as Opportunity's “My battery is low and it's getting dark”, which was a journalist's summary, not a real message. That's a good discussion about checking sources.

Printable worksheets

Each worksheet prints on its own page(s). Use “Print” on a worksheet to print just that one.

Adopt a Ghost: paste your class list and print an adoption certificate for every student, each with a different machine. A good end-of-unit activity or a hook for a research project on “their” machine.
